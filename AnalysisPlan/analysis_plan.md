# Analysis plan

**Study:** Food affordability and the household burden of health spending in Türkiye (Income and Living Conditions Survey, 2023–2025 cross-sections)
**Authors:** Salim Yılmaz, Ahmet Murat Günal
**Status of this document:** This plan was written in October 2026, before any model was fitted to the 2025 data, as the working protocol of the study. It was not registered. For deposit it was translated from Turkish into English and reformatted; the hypotheses, estimands, variable definitions, covariate rules, model-building protocol, analysis stages, sensitivity list and simulation design are reproduced as written. Operational details that concern only the computing environment (file paths, hardware, run order) were removed. Departures from the plan that were decided after the first results had been inspected are listed in Section 10; they are also stated in the manuscript.

## 1. Data and scope

- Source: Turkish Statistical Institute (TurkStat), Income and Living Conditions Survey (ILCS), cross-sectional microdata sets for 2023, 2024 and 2025 (239,985 person rows in total). Identifiers are reassigned every year, so the three cross-sections are repeated cross-sections and are not linked.
- The 2025 cross-section carries the health module (financial burden of medical care, medicines and dental care; fruit and vegetable frequency; physician visits; body mass index) and a TurkStat item on meat, chicken or fish frequency. The food affordability item (HE090) and the unmet-need items exist in all three years.
- Main analyses use 2025 only. The 2023 and 2024 cross-sections are used only for descriptive context, each year analyzed separately with its own weights; no pooling across years.
- Target journal: Foods (MDPI). Language of tables and figures: English.

## 2. Research question and hypotheses

Central question: compared with households at the same income level, what do households that cannot afford a meal with meat, chicken or fish every second day eat, and how do they carry health spending?

**Hypothesis 1 (main; household level).** The adjusted prevalence difference between households that cannot (A = 1) and can (A = 0) afford the meal in the outcome "medical care or medicine expenditure was a heavy burden for the household" (Y) is larger in households with at least one adult with chronic illness (C = 1). The estimand is the additive interaction built from four standardized cell prevalences, Δ = p11 − p10 − p01 + p00, with the expected direction Δ > 0. The coefficient or p value of the product term in a logistic model is not the test of this hypothesis; it is reported for comparison only. RERI, the attributable proportion and prevalence ratios are reported alongside Δ.

**Hypothesis 2 (adult level).** Adults aged 18 years and older living in A = 1 households consume meat, chicken or fish, fruit, and vegetables less frequently than other adults at the same equivalized income quintile and other covariates. Whether the gradient differs between food groups is exploratory. No wording implying temporal order ("which food is given up first") is used.

**Supporting analysis (latent class analysis).** Adult-level latent class analysis with the affordability item and the three consumption frequencies as indicators, to describe how affordability and consumption frequency combine and how the spending burden is distributed across the profiles (whether it follows the affordability dimension or the consumption dimension). This is a plausibility check at the pattern level, not a causal separation. The expected profiles (affording and frequent; affording but infrequent; not affording and infrequent) may not emerge; whatever emerges is reported.

**Secondary outcomes.** Unmet medical or dental need because of cost; no specialist visit in the past 12 months among adults with chronic illness; distribution of the main reason for unmet need (cost, system-related, personal). Dental expenditure burden is separate and secondary.

**Exploratory analysis (earlier hypothesis).** The association of the combination of A with infrequent fruit and vegetable consumption (B) with unmet medical need because of cost. Only 340 persons report FS060 = 1 in 2025, so this is not a main test; cell counts, event counts and precision are reported with it.

**Context analysis.** The 2023–2025 course of HE090 by income quintile, region and household type.

**Simulation.** Precision and power of the Δ estimator on the real design and covariate distribution; behaviour of the estimator under the additive null; effect of A-dependent reporting bias (additive shift or multiplicative) on Δ; effect of household clustering at the adult level.

## 3. Analytic data sets

- Adult data set: 2025, persons with an individual questionnaire and age (FK070, age on 31 December of the income reference year) of 18 years or more. Weight FB030. Cluster: household.
- Household data set: 2025, one row per household (household reference person row). Weight HB040. Household counts (number of adults; adults with chronic illness; adults without an individual record, whose chronic-illness status is unknown; adults with activity limitation; employed adults; highest education among adults; age and sex of the reference person) are computed from all 2025 rows of the household.
- Age threshold: FK070 ≥ 18; persons who turned 18 between the reference date and the interview are excluded, which is stated in the Methods. FK070 ≥ 17 is a sensitivity analysis.
- Income refers to the previous calendar year; across years only within-year relative measures (equivalized income quintile, at-risk-of-poverty indicator) are used.
- The microdata contain no stratum or primary sampling unit identifiers; variance estimation uses household clustering, and NUTS-2 regions serve as pseudo-strata in a sensitivity analysis.

## 5. Covariates

### 5.1 Covariate pool

Adult level: age, sex, education (five groups), marital status, current activity status, household type, household size, members aged under 18 and 65 or older, NUTS-1 region, equivalized income quintile, log equivalized income, housing tenure, chronic illness, activity limitation (GALI), self-rated health, disability (Washington Group Short Set).

Household level: income quintile, number of adults, members aged under 18 and 65 or older, household type, age and sex of the reference person, highest education among adults, number of employed adults, number of adults with activity limitation, NUTS-1 region, housing tenure.

Never in the main model, and only in sensitivity analyses: the household's ability to make ends meet (HE040), which measures the same subjective budget perception as A and may lie on the path from A to Y; the capacity to face unexpected expenses (HE100), material deprivation counts and housing cost overburden, which overlap with A as deprivation indicators.

### 5.2 Covariate selection protocol

Principle: covariates are selected by their role as confounders, not by statistical significance; the selection is tested against the data and every step is reported. No stepwise selection by p values. Three tiers:

- Tier 1, core: variables that are common causes of A and the outcome by design and enter every model. Household model: income quintile, number of adults, members aged under 18, members aged 65 or older, age and sex of the reference person, highest education among adults, region. Adult models: age, sex, education, income quintile, region, household size, members aged under 18.
- Tier 2, need indicators: in models whose outcome is health spending or health care use, because health care need is the strongest determinant of these outcomes and a confounder if related to A. Household model: number of adults with activity limitation (C itself is in the model; its count version is a sensitivity analysis). Adult model: own activity limitation; self-rated health is tested as a candidate. In the dietary models (Hypothesis 2) chronic illness and activity limitation are candidates, because illness may change the diet but is not a cause of A.
- Tier 3, candidates: number of employed adults or activity status, housing tenure, household type, marital status, disability, self-rated health. Each candidate is added one at a time to the core (plus need) model and judged by two criteria: (a) change in estimate, decisive: the candidate enters the final model if the absolute change in Δ is at least 0.25 percentage points or the relative change in the standardized prevalence ratio of A is at least 10%; in the dietary models the criterion is 0.5 points in the prevalence difference of A or 10% in its ratio; (b) design-based block Wald test, supporting: a candidate with p < 0.05 that does not meet the change criterion may be included if it reduces the standard error of Δ by more than 5%, and this is reported separately. After all candidates have been tested singly, the selected ones are added jointly and Δ is recomputed; a candidate that no longer meets the criterion in the joint model is nevertheless kept (no backward elimination). A table reports, for each candidate, Δ before and after, PR before and after, change in SE, Wald p and decision.
- Collinearity: pairs with GVIF^(1/(2·df)) > 2.2 in the final model are examined and one conceptually overlapping variable is dropped and reported.
- Sparse categories: a category with unweighted n < 100 or fewer than 10 events is merged with its neighbour and the rule is reported.
- Exploratory earlier hypothesis (340 events): a reduced Tier 1 only (age, sex, income quintile, region); if events per parameter fall below 10, region is dropped or Firth correction is applied.
- Reporting: three model columns are always produced and compared: minimal (age, sex, income quintile), core plus need, and final (with the selected candidates). The main table reports the final model and a supplementary table all three. If Δ is similar across the three models this stability is reported; if not, the covariate that produces the difference is identified and reported.

## 6. Survey design, weights and variance

R survey package. Adult level: household clusters, weight FB030. Household level: no clustering, weight HB040. Sensitivity: NUTS-2 pseudo-strata. All estimates weighted; tables report unweighted n with weighted percentages. Years analyzed separately.

Confidence intervals for the main results: household-level resampling bootstrap (Rao–Wu–Yue–Beaumont rescaling), 1,000 replicates, 2,000 if time allows; the model is refitted and the standardized prevalences recomputed in every replicate; percentile intervals. Delta-method (linearization) standard errors are computed alongside for comparison.

## 7. Model-building and assumption-checking protocol

Applied before every main model of Stages 3 and 5, in this order; the result of every check and the decision taken are recorded as "check, result, decision", with a reason wherever the rule is not followed.

1. Missing data. Complete-case analysis if the loss of complete cases is below 5%; multiple imputation (m = 20, with weight, cluster and all model variables in the imputation model, Rubin's rules, standardized prevalences computed in each imputation and combined) as the main analysis if the loss is 5–20%, with complete cases as sensitivity; above 20% the variable's place in the model is reconsidered. Routing blanks in the module visit counts are not missing values but rule-based codes; both codings are reported.
2. Functional form. Continuous covariates (age, age of the reference person, log equivalized income, diet score) enter first as natural splines with 4 degrees of freedom. Design-based Wald test of the nonlinear components and comparison of Δ between the linear and the spline model: the linear form is used if the nonlinear components have p ≥ 0.10 and Δ differs by less than 0.2 points; otherwise the spline is kept. For age, 3, 4 and 5 degrees of freedom are tried; 4 is used if Δ does not change.
3. Overlap and positivity. Distribution of the four A × C cells across income quintile × region × education strata. If strata are empty or have unweighted n < 20, the standardization target is restricted to overlapping strata or categories are merged; which was done, and the population share left out, are reported.
4. Link function and model form. The main estimator is logistic regression with g-computation. The same Δ is also computed from a weighted linear probability model (household-clustered robust SE; the A × C coefficient is a direct estimate of Δ) and from a flexible logistic model that includes the interactions of A with every core covariate, followed by g-computation. If the three estimates differ by less than 0.5 points the main model is kept and all three are reported in the supplement; if they differ by more, the flexible model becomes the main model and the reason is reported.
5. Goodness of fit. Archer–Lemeshow F-corrected test for the survey logistic model and observed-versus-predicted calibration in deciles. If fit is poor, functional form, sparse categories and interactions are reviewed in that order; if fit does not improve, the flexible model of step 4 is used.
6. Effect-modification screen. For the main outcome, the interactions A × income quintile, A × region and A × sex of the reference person are tested one at a time (design-based Wald test). If p < 0.01 and the cells have enough events, stratified Δ is reported in a supplementary table; the main Δ remains the population average. The screen is exploratory and is reported as such.
7. Separation and sparsity. Events per parameter for every model; if below 10 the model is reduced; complete or quasi-complete separation leads to dropping the variable or comparison with a Firth-corrected (unweighted) model. A parameter whose standard error exceeds five times its coefficient is treated as a sign of separation.
8. Weight distribution. Distribution of the weights, ratio of the maximum to the median, effective sample size. If the ratio exceeds 10, a sensitivity analysis with weights trimmed at the 1st and 99th percentiles.
9. Ordinal models. For the proportional odds model the assumption is checked by fitting a separate binary logistic model at every cut point and comparing the odds ratios of A. If the odds ratios differ by more than 30% or their confidence intervals do not overlap, a partial proportional odds model or cut-point-specific binary outcomes become the main report and the proportional odds result goes to the supplement.
10. Latent class analysis. Local independence is checked with bivariate residuals (indicator pairs with a standardized residual above 3). If residual dependence between fruit and vegetables remains, the options are, in order, one more class, then merging the two indicators into one fruit and vegetable score. The best log-likelihood must be replicated in at least 20% of the random starts; otherwise the number of starts is increased. If entropy is below 0.6 the classes are still used (BCH corrects for classification error) but this low separation is reported and differences in distal outcomes are interpreted with caution.
11. Clustering. The design effect of household clustering is reported for every main estimate, with the reduction in the standard error when clustering is ignored.
12. Bootstrap convergence. Confidence limits are compared at 500, 1,000 and 2,000 replicates; if the limits move by more than 0.1 points between 1,000 and 2,000 replicates, the number of replicates is increased.

All outputs of the protocol are collected in one table: check, criterion, result, decision, affected model.

## 8. Analysis stages

**Stage 1. Set-up, reading, validation.** Validation of the data against published TurkStat and Eurostat figures (row and column counts, counts of key codes, weighted totals, reproduction of the at-risk-of-poverty rate, the Gini coefficient and the unmet medical need indicator). Variable definitions are written to a file.

**Stage 2. Preliminary checks and descriptive analysis.** Distribution and overlap of the Y components; the A × C cells (unweighted n, weighted share) at household and adult level; households with adults of unknown chronic status; the A × B cells and events per cell for the earlier hypothesis; search for a household-respondent identifier (if none, the reference person is used as a proxy and this is reported); vegetarians; missing data in the covariate pool; weight distribution; category sizes and event counts. For each cell the minimum detectable Δ at 80% power (one-sided 5%) is computed from the observed standard error and reported; the plan continues regardless. Descriptive outputs: Table 1 (adults by A), household characteristics by A, the 2023–2025 context of HE090 at household and person level (the person-level series is the one comparable with Eurostat ilc_mdes03) by income quintile, region (with the TR63 note; 2023 lacks TR63, so a 25-region comparison is also computed) and household type, consumption frequency distributions by A and income quintile, and the reasons for unmet need by A across 2023–2025.

**Stage 3. Hypothesis 2, dietary models.** Covariate selection and the protocol are applied first; the covariate set is then fixed. For the three binary outcomes: survey-weighted logistic regression (quasibinomial), A plus covariates; g-computation gives standardized prevalences under A = 1 and A = 0, their difference (percentage points) and ratio, with bootstrap intervals. For the three ordinal outcomes: survey-weighted proportional odds models with the proportional odds check. Income-quintile-specific standardized differences from the model with A × income quintile are produced for the figure. Prevalence ratios of the food groups are shown side by side; a formal comparison, if made, uses paired bootstrap differences and is labelled exploratory.

**Stage 4. Latent class analysis.** StepMix (weights and three-step BCH supported), with an unweighted poLCA cross-check. Indicators: HE090 (2 categories), fruit and vegetables (6 categories collapsed to 4: never or less than weekly / 1–3 per week / 4–6 per week / daily), meat, chicken or fish (4 categories; code 0 merged with code 1 if sparse). Population: adults with complete data on the four indicators; weight FB030. One to six classes; at least 50 random starts; table of log-likelihood, BIC, aBIC, CAIC, entropy and smallest class share; bootstrap likelihood ratio test if feasible. Selection: elbow of the BIC/aBIC curve, entropy, smallest class above 5%, interpretability; the neighbouring solutions with one class fewer and one more are reported in the supplement. Outputs: item-response probabilities, weighted class shares, posterior probabilities. Three-step BCH for distal outcomes: household heavy burden (linked to each adult), unmet medical need because of cost, bad or very bad self-rated health; covariates income quintile, age, sex, education, region, household type. Multinomial model of class membership on covariates. The adult-level estimate means "what share of adults in a profile live in a high-burden household"; it is not the same estimand as the household analysis, and this is stated.

**Stage 5. Hypothesis 1 and related analyses.** Covariate selection and the protocol are applied to the household model; the final model is the result of the protocol. The minimal, core plus need and final models are all kept. Main model: survey-weighted logistic regression of Y on A, C, A × C and covariates; g-computation over the four (A, C) scenarios with HB040-weighted averaging gives p00, p01, p10, p11. Reported: unweighted n and events of the four cells, crude weighted prevalences, standardized prevalences, Δ, RERI = Δ/p00, AP = Δ/p11, prevalence ratios PR10, PR01, PR11, the product-term odds ratio (comparison only), all with bootstrap intervals, and the minimum detectable Δ from the observed standard error. Adult-level repetition: Y linked to each adult, the adult's own chronic illness as modifier, FB030, household clustering, adult covariates. Household-respondent check: among adults who are not the household reference person, the association of A with outcomes from the individual questionnaire (unmet medical and dental need because of cost, no specialist visit), as a check on common-method bias. Specificity: latent classes as exposure and Y as outcome (BCH, with covariates); the main comparison is between the "cannot afford" classes and the "can afford but infrequent" class; if such classes do not emerge, the closest available comparison is made and the reason stated. Secondary: unmet need by A and C among adults with a need; no specialist visit among adults with chronic illness by A; dental burden by A, with code 4 separate. Exploratory earlier hypothesis: among adults with a need, unmet medical need because of cost on A × B plus covariates, four standardized cell prevalences and Δ with bootstrap intervals; a cell with fewer than 20 unweighted events is not estimated and this is reported; ordinal version with A × diet score and predicted prevalence curves. Model diagnostics (separation, events per parameter, effective sample size) with Firth or a smaller covariate set if needed.

**Stage 6. Sensitivity analyses (pre-specified list; one row each in a supplementary table).** (1) HE040 added to the model; (2) the broad outcome (heavy or some burden); (3) households with MODUL1_H_1 = 4 excluded; (4) alternative definitions of C: number of adults with chronic illness (0 / 1 / 2+), proportion of adults with chronic illness, presence of an adult with activity limitation (GALI); (5) FK070 ≥ 17; (6) vegetarians included in the meat outcome as less than weekly; (7) visit-count blanks counted as no visit; (8) NUTS-2 pseudo-stratified design; (9) log equivalized income spline instead of quintiles; (10) without household clustering (for illustration only; documents the reduction in the standard error); (11) region specification alternatives (region is a fixed effect; a model without region and, if available, another regional alternative). Results are written with the main estimate into a forest-plot data set.

**Stage 7. Plasmode simulation.** Purpose: four questions. What size of Δ does this design estimate with what precision? When the true Δ is zero, do the estimator and its interval behave correctly, and how does the product-term test diverge from the additive test? How does A-dependent reporting bias affect Δ? What happens at the adult level if household clustering is ignored? Design: the real household data set (covariates, A, C, weights) is held fixed as the population and only Y is generated, on the additive scale so that the true Δ is controlled exactly: p_i = base_i + βA·A_i + βC·C_i + Δ·A_i·C_i, where base_i is a covariate-dependent baseline probability (predicted from a model without the product term), βA and βC are calibrated to the observed cell differences (p10 − p00, p01 − p00), and p_i is truncated to [0.005, 0.95]. Setting the interaction coefficient of a logistic model to zero is not the additive null; the null scenario is built so that Δ = 0. Scenario dimensions: true Δ 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 points; reporting bias none, additive shift (false-positive probability δ among A = 1 households, about 0.02) or multiplicative (p_i × (1 + γ) among A = 1 households, about γ = 0.3), with values chosen to be plausible relative to the observed prevalences; clustering (adult-level scenarios only) through a household random effect with ICC 0.3 and 0.6, analyzed with and without clustering. In each replicate: generate Y, apply the main estimator (weighted quasibinomial model with covariates and A × C, g-computation), record Δ̂, its delta-method standard error and interval; bootstrap intervals are compared with delta-method intervals in a 200-replicate subset of the Δ = 0 and Δ = 2 scenarios only. Performance measures: bias, empirical SE, mean model SE, coverage (95%), rejection rate (type I error at Δ = 0, power otherwise), rejection rate of the product-term test. Replicates: a 50-replicate pilot per scenario, then 2,000 replicates for the Δ = 0 scenarios and 1,000 otherwise; seeds recorded.

**Stage 8. Figures.** Figure 1 study design (sample flow and analytic framework); Figure 2 context and diet (HE090 trend 2023–2025 by income quintile with the EU reference, consumption frequency distributions by A, standardized differences overall and by income quintile); Figure 3 latent classes (item-response profiles, class shares by income quintile and by region); Figure 4 main result (four standardized cell prevalences with the additive expectation at household and adult level, burden by latent class, forest plot of the sensitivity analyses); Figure 5 simulation (power and type I error of the additive and product-term tests, bias under reporting bias, coverage, SE ratio under clustering). Supplementary figures: reasons for unmet need by A over 2023–2025; LCA fit indices by number of classes; the four cells of the earlier hypothesis and the ordinal B curves; module routing blanks and missingness. Figures are drawn from saved data files and do not recompute results.

**Stage 9. Tables.** Table 1 characteristics of adults by A; Table 2 dietary frequency by A (standardized prevalences, differences, ratios, ordinal odds ratios; income strata may move to the supplement); Table 3 household burden by A and C (four cells with n, events, crude and standardized prevalences; Δ, RERI, AP, product-term OR; household and adult blocks). Supplementary tables: household characteristics by A; LCA fit; LCA item-response probabilities and class shares; distal outcomes by class; unmet need and reasons; sensitivity analyses; simulation summary; variable definitions; earlier-hypothesis cells; household-respondent check.

## 9. Reporting rules and data protection

- Microdata do not leave the project folder and are never copied to a synchronized location. Outputs contain aggregated statistics only. Estimates based on unweighted cell counts below 20 are not shown ("<20"); cells with 20–50 are flagged. No file with person-level rows is written to the output folders.
- Unweighted n and weighted percentages are reported together and labelled as such. Years are never pooled.
- Every TurkStat and Eurostat source is cited; the manuscript states the age definition (31 December of the income reference year), the income reference year and the absence of strata and PSU identifiers.

## 10. Departures from the plan decided after the first results had been inspected

1. **Model-form rule (protocol step 4).** The original rule chose the main model by the difference between the logistic, linear probability and flexible logistic estimates (threshold 0.5 points). After the first results had been inspected, the rule was refined: the flexible specification is the main model only where the effect-modification screen (step 6) indicates heterogeneity at p < 0.01; otherwise the main-effects model is the main model and the flexible and linear probability models are reported as sensitivity analyses. Under this rule the flexible model is the main model for the three dietary outcomes and the main-effects model for Hypothesis 1. Under the original rule the flexible model with the core plus need covariates would have been the main model for Hypothesis 1 as well; both estimates are reported.
2. **Main covariate set for Hypothesis 1.** The protocol would have made the model with the candidates selected by the change-in-estimate rule (numbers of adults with disability and with bad self-rated health; at the adult level own self-rated health) the main model. Because these variables may lie on the pathway from chronic illness to spending burden, the core plus need model was designated the main model after the results had been seen, and the model with the selected variables is reported as the fully adjusted step of the adjustment ladder.
3. **Activity-limitation modifier, second column.** A model with the presence of an adult with activity limitation as modifier and additional adjustment for the number of adults with chronic illness was added after the first activity-limitation estimate had been inspected.
4. **Core model without the need indicator.** A model with all core sociodemographic covariates and no need indicator was added to the adjustment ladder during manuscript revision, as an intermediate step between the minimal and the core plus need model.
5. **Bootstrap replicates.** The main estimates use 4,000 replicates (protocol step 12 allowed an increase when limits moved by more than 0.1 points; the fruit difference moved by 0.14 points between 1,000 and 2,000 replicates); other analyses use 2,000.
6. **Overlap (protocol step 3).** The main estimates were standardized to the whole analytic sample; the estimate restricted to overlapping fine strata was reported as a sensitivity analysis, together with two coarse overlap checks (income × region; income × education) that were added during the analysis.
7. **Latent class indicators (protocol step 10).** Because of local dependence between the affordability item and meat frequency, the main model used a joint six-category affordability-by-meat indicator; the model with four separate indicators was kept as a sensitivity model. The protocol foresaw local dependence between fruit and vegetables; the observed dependence concerned a different pair, and the merging option of step 10 was applied to that pair.
8. **Multiple imputation** was not needed (complete-case loss 0% for adults and 0.05% for households). **Weight trimming** was not needed (ratio of maximum to median 3.6). **Partial proportional odds** was not fitted; where the proportional odds assumption failed (vegetables), the cut-point-specific odds ratios were reported as planned.
